
The tradition centers on the four purusharthas—dharma (righteous conduct), artha (material well‑being), kama (desire/pleasure), and moksha (liberation from rebirth). These aims are presented as a balanced framework for personal fulfillment and social harmony. [source needed]

Unlike religions that rely on a single holy book, Sanātana Dharma draws on a wide corpus: the Vedas, Upanishads, Bhagavad‑Gītā, Dharma‑śāstras such as Manusmṛti, and extensive bhakti literature. This pluralistic base permits regional and sectarian variation while preserving a shared philosophical core. [source needed]

Historically, dharma informed the dharma‑śāstra tradition, which regulated personal law, caste duties, and state policy in pre‑colonial India; its influence resurfaces in contemporary constitutional debates about India’s cultural heritage. [source needed]

Since the late 20th century, some Hindu revivalist and diaspora groups have promoted “Sanātana Dharma” as a preferred self‑identifier over “Hinduism,” arguing it better reflects the tradition’s universal claim.
