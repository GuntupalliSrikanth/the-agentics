Sanātana Dharma (“eternal law”) is the self‑designated name ancient practitioners used for the religious‑philosophical system later labeled Hinduism [source needed]. The phrase occurs in classical Sanskrit works such as the *Manusmṛti* (c. 2nd century CE) and the *Bhagavad Gītā* (c. 2nd century BCE–1st century CE), where it refers to timeless duties rather than a fixed creed [source needed]. It does not appear in the earliest Rig‑Veda hymns, which invoke many deities without a single umbrella term [source needed].

Sanātana Dharma foregrounds four interrelated concepts: dharma (righteous conduct), karma (action and its results), moksha (liberation from rebirth), and yajña (self‑offering or sacrifice). Texts present these as universal principles applicable to all beings, irrespective of caste, gender, or era [source needed]. Different philosophical schools interpret them variously—Advaita Vedānta sees ultimate reality as the impersonal Brahman, Dvaita Vedānta as a personal Ishvara, and other traditions blend both views [source needed]. This theological pluralism is a core feature, not a later addition [source needed].

In contemporary India the term is frequently invoked by groups such as the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh and ISKCON to assert an unbroken heritage [source needed]. Scholars like Wendy Doniger and Patrick Olivelle argue that “Sanātana Dharma” functions more as an aspirational ideal than a concrete doctrinal list, and that equating it directly with “Hinduism” oversimplifies the tradition’s internal diversity [source needed]. Thus, while early texts confirm the phrase’s historic use, its modern political deployment adds interpretive layers that differ from the original textual context [source needed].
